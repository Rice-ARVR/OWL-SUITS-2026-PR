SUIT OXYGEN SYSTEM

The oxygen tanks are filled during the UIA egress procedure before the EVA begins. Oxygen is consumed continuously by the astronaut throughout the EVA. The primary oxygen tank feeds the suit atmosphere and is expected to deplete gradually over the mission duration. The secondary oxygen tank is a backup and should remain full unless the primary has failed or been exhausted.

A declining suit_pressure_oxy reading means the primary oxygen supply is not maintaining the target partial pressure of 4.0 psi. This can happen because the primary tank is running low, the regulator is malfunctioning, or there is a supply line issue. The suit atmosphere partial pressure of oxygen must be kept near 4.0 psi — too low causes hypoxia, too high increases flammability risk.

A declining oxy_pri_storage percentage confirms the primary tank is depleting. If the rate of depletion is faster than expected given the astronaut's oxy_consumption rate, this suggests a leak rather than normal use.

SUIT CO2 SCRUBBER SYSTEM

The astronaut continuously produces carbon dioxide as a metabolic byproduct. CO2 is collected by chemical scrubbers inside the suit. Each suit has two scrubbers — scrubber A and scrubber B. Only one is active at a time; the other is being vented to space. The active scrubber fills over the course of the EVA, which is expected and normal. When a scrubber approaches full capacity (60%), the astronaut must switch to the empty scrubber so the full one can vent.

A rising suit_pressure_co2 reading means CO2 is accumulating in the suit atmosphere. This happens when the active scrubber is too full to collect CO2 efficiently. If suit_pressure_co2 exceeds 0.1 psi, the scrubber is the cause.

A rising scrubber_a_co2_storage or scrubber_b_co2_storage percentage is normal during EVA. It becomes a problem at 60%, at which point efficiency drops rapidly and CO2 will begin passing through into the suit atmosphere.

HELMET CO2 POOLING AND FANS

Carbon dioxide is denser than oxygen and tends to pool in low-lying areas of the suit, particularly the helmet bubble, due to the astronaut's breathing pattern. Fans inside the helmet push CO2 away from the breathing zone toward the scrubbers in the rest of the suit.

Each suit has a primary and secondary fan. Only one fan runs at a time. Both fans should spin at 30,000 RPM when active. A fan running below this speed moves less air, which reduces CO2 circulation efficiency.

A rising helmet_pressure_co2 reading is the direct result of insufficient CO2 circulation — almost always caused by a fan running too slowly or having failed entirely. If helmet_pressure_co2 is rising while suit_pressure_co2 is stable or low, the scrubber itself is not the problem — the CO2 is pooling in the helmet before reaching the scrubber. Helmet CO2 above 0.15 psi can impair cognitive function and must be treated immediately.

A low fan_pri_rpm or fan_sec_rpm reading (below 30,000 RPM while that fan is active) confirms mechanical fan degradation or failure. Trends showing a gradual RPM decline suggest a fan bearing or motor issue developing over time.

SUIT ATMOSPHERE — OTHER GASES

After the suit decompression sequence during egress, all gases other than oxygen should be purged from the suit atmosphere. The suit_pressure_other field reflects the partial pressure of all non-oxygen, non-CO2 gases remaining in the suit. This value should be 0.0 psi once decompression is complete.

A non-zero suit_pressure_other reading after egress indicates incomplete decompression or atmospheric contamination. This is not a routine occurrence and represents a suit integrity or procedure concern.

---

SUIT TOTAL PRESSURE

The suit_pressure_total value is the sum of all partial pressures inside the suit: oxygen, CO2, and other gases. The target is approximately 4.0 psi, matching the oxygen partial pressure target since CO2 and other gases should be near zero.

A declining suit_pressure_total that is not explained by suit_pressure_co2 or suit_pressure_other changes points to an oxygen supply problem — the total pressure is falling because less oxygen is entering the suit than is being consumed. A rising suit_pressure_total means either CO2 is accumulating (scrubber issue) or other gases are present (contamination or incomplete depress).

SUIT TEMPERATURE AND THERMAL CONTROL

The lunar environment creates extreme thermal conditions — direct sunlight surfaces can exceed 120°C while shadowed surfaces drop to -170°C. The suit is well insulated, which means the astronaut's own metabolic heat can accumulate inside the suit during physical exertion.

A rising temperature reading during high physical activity is expected. It becomes a concern above 32°C. The suit uses a coolant loop to manage internal temperature — coolant absorbs body heat and a radiator dissipates it. The coolant is filled during egress and is a self-contained closed system.

A declining coolant_storage value is abnormal. Since the coolant loop is sealed and self-recycling, any loss of coolant indicates a breach in the loop. This would reduce the suit's ability to shed heat, causing temperature to rise faster than normal during activity.

---

HEART RATE

The astronaut's heart rate reflects physical exertion and physiological stress. On the lunar surface, reduced gravity (1/6 of Earth's) can make movement feel easy while still requiring significant muscular effort, particularly in a pressurized suit. Heat buildup inside the suit, dehydration, and high workload all contribute to elevated heart rate.

A sustained rising trend in heart_rate, especially when paired with a rising temperature, indicates the astronaut is exerting heavily or the suit's thermal management is struggling. A sudden spike in heart rate without a change in activity may reflect stress or a suit comfort issue.

---

BATTERY SYSTEM

The suit battery powers all electronic systems including fans, sensors, and communications. The primary battery level depletes throughout the EVA. The secondary battery is a backup. Battery depletion follows a predictable curve based on suit system load.

An unusually fast battery drain rate may indicate a system drawing more power than expected, such as a fan running at high load. Battery levels below 20% are outside the nominal range and require attention.
